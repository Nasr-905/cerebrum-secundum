Today is June 2nd 2021 and this is my Individual Oral for SL Language and Literature

  

My global issue identifies the effect of refusing to adapt or conform to social norms on integration into a new environment. I’ll be exploring this concept through My nonliterary work: Minding The Gap and my literary work: The Nickel Boys.

  

Minding the Gap is a documentary released in 2018, that explores the difficulty of progressing into adulthood for America's neglected youth. The teens in the documentary are at times ignorant to their childish behavior, but throughout the film, they begin to realise a new adult environment where they are conflicted with societal expectations. Some of which they deny and others they accept in their transition out of adolescence.

  

The word choice, shot selection and lighting in the film convey their acceptance or denial of these ‘adult’ stereotypes. And thus, communicating the message that one does not need to completely conform to social norms to integrate into a new environment

  

In my extract, Bing Liu quickly clips between scenes consisting of his interview with his mom, the conflict between Nina and Zack, and Kiere’s relationship with his father.

  

In his interview, Bing and his mom only refer to his step-dad indirectly as ‘him’, and never as dad, or by name. This choice of words gives the audience the impression that Bing and his mother don’t see his step-father as family at all.

In the interview, Bing can be seen behind his filming equipment in poor lighting. This adds to the authenticity and intimacy of the interview as the audience feels like they are sitting right next to them experiencing the conversation unfold.

Bing follows by saying He gets “shaky and anxious” when he thinks about him so he tries not to think about him at all. To which his mom says: “If you try to don’t think about it… I don’t think that’s gonna work… you cannot change the past”. Which perpetuates the idea that as an adult, Bing should “move on and not dwell in the past” as Bing himself put it. The global issue is explored here in the way Bing accepts the idea of moving on as a part of adulthood.

  

Similarly Zack struggles between two opposing viewpoints. He says: “On one hand of my brain i'm like: “You shouldn’t be =garound your kid at all, you fucking suck!”, and then the other hand I’m like: “No! That’s my child!”. The former represents the opinion of those that see him as ‘bad influence’ and the latter reassures himself that he’s not the kind of person. These statements are dubbed over clips of Nina and their son. These clips are taken almost covertly in a lurking position with a form of obstruction in the foreground. The effect of this shot selection over Zack’s voice gives the feeling of Zack’s separation from his son and ex-girl friend.

  

This scene in particular helps the audience understand Zack and his opposition to the expected social norms. In essence how he refuses to distance himself from his child simply because some may see him as a bad influence. And in this way, Zack speaks directly to the global issue.

  

The importance of this extract is seen in part by Bing Liu’s interview with his mother and marks a turning point in Bing Liu’s development as a character because it finally allowed him to face his mother and speak about their trauma and abuses at the hand of his step father.

  

The extract also shows the point where Zack gives context to his past actions which serves to tie up loose ends in his part of the documentary.

  

This point in the film has a greater holistic meaning that we understand which expectations of the adult environment they accept and which they deny. Earlier the characters identified these such expectations, Zack in particular said: “I’m like my dad: there’s no such thing as emotion, only work and getting back to it. If you work hard enough, all your problems will go away”. Later, in my extract, he accepts this expectation because he doesn’t want his son to grow up like him.

  

Authorship of Minding the Gap is credited to Bing Liu as the director of the documentary as well as the primary filmer.

He began recording himself and friends from a young age with the intent of documenting a life similar to his own growing up through Kiere and Zack.

Bing choosing to convey the story as nonfiction adds authenticity and credibility to the effect of expanding the story’s significance beyond the small town of Rockford and into an ‘American experience’ as he explains in an interview on the daily show.

  

Bing Liu is also able to expand the sense of time beyond the 5 years he spent filming for the documentary by using clips he’s taken from their younger skateboarding days almost 12 years ago. This adds a story-like effect that takes the audience intimately into the lives of the characters, almost as though they were fictional. This allowed the audience to better understand the story’s themes and their adult environment.

  

Similarly, my literary work: The Nickel boys also recognizes my global issue.

The novel by Christian Whitehead surrounds Elwood, an african american teen in the 1960s, brought up in an environment that rewarded integrity and hard-work. Later, he was arrested and taken to Nickel Academy, an environment completely opposed to his ideologies. Despite experiencing abuse he continued to live by his own morals, until he died by his words.

  

Colson Whitehead uses repetition, tone, and narration techniques in his work to amplify Elwood’s rejection of Nickel’s norms and, by extension, the author’s exploration of the global issue.

  

In the Nickel Boys’ extract I chose Turner discovers Elwood’s journals and his intent to share them with the state inspectors and disclose Nickel’s abuses.

  

When Turner first questions Elwood about these journals he speaks matter-of-factly through short and succinct phrases; he says: 

  

“‘I’ve been writing everything down’… The deliveries. The yard work and chores. The names of everybody and the dates. All our Community Service’”

  

This is done in complete contrast to Turner's visible outrage.

  

Elwood’s cadence appeals to logos as he speaks as though his actions were without consequences and completely logical which gives the audience a sense of Elwood’s boldness in an act so unthinkable by most Nickle Boys.

Furthermore, Whitehead uses Turner as a means of strengthening Elwood’s resolve in the eyes of the audience. This is done by Turner asking similar questions to those the reader may be asking in the following quote: “What do you think they going to do? Put your picture on the cover of Time magazine?”

Elwood simply replies by saying “I did it to stop it”.

  

This narrative technique serves to answer the questions the reader may have. And the use of the past tense in his replies expresses Elwood’s certainty in his resolve to bring justice to Nickel.

Turner also echoes the questions of the reader when he asks why Elwood would risk losing the progress he’s made in integrating into Nickel over a couple journals. This question breaks Elwood pragmatic, matter-of-fact tone and he begins to reply in a metaphor; he says:

“You’re wrong, Turner… It’s not an obstacle course… You can’t go around it—you have to go through it. Walk with your head up no matter what they throw at you.”

  

This statement is directed at the audience; as a model of life through oppression. Elwood switches from an appeal to logos to a more metaphorical appeal to pathos. And Thus, Elwood speaks directly to the Global Issue as he preaches against integration at the expense of his morals and beliefs.

  

Elwood’s metaphor implies that integration is not compulsory when transitioning into a new environment, in fact refusing to conform draws attention to you and your differing morals.

  

This extract is a developmental climax in the Nickel Boys as Elwood is able to express all of the suffering, abuse and opposition he endured behind Nickel walls.

Earlier in the novel, Elwood struggled to find the words to explain himself and his relentless sense of social justice;

“Until At Zion HIll gave him a language”. This was a record he owned of Martin Luthar King which stated: “We must believe in our souls that we are somebody, that we are significant, that we are worthful, and we must walk the streets of life every day with this sense of dignity and this sense of somebody-ness”. This record had such a profound impact on Elwood, that Martin Luthar King became a recurring symbol of activism in the novel.

  

The extract also displays a point of affirmation for Elwood and a point of realization for Turner, that has continued significance later in the novel where Turner inherits Elwood’s will by delivering the journals to the inspectors on his behalf.

This turning point in Turner's ideology stemmed from Elwood’s opposition to moral change. This relates to the global issue as Elwood proves that refusing to integrate into an oppressive environment has the ability to influence even those most set in their ways.

  

Moreover, the Nickel Boys adds another book to Whitehead’s catalog of novels that touch on race, oppression, and societal belonging.

In an interview on his award winning novel: the underground railroad, he provided a call to action saying: “Be kind to everybody. Make art. Fight the power”.This statement was made mere weeks after Donald Trump came into office and served as opposition against the administration, the government and the “power” he references.

Even outside his works, Whitehead applies the global issue by using his societal opposition as a platform for his activism

  

All in all both texts uniquely exemplify the global issue. In the Nickel Boys the reader is exposed to the fact that opposition against moral change in a new environment will allow you to be better heard. While Minding the gap, highlights the transition into a new social environment and the importance of deciding for yourself which societal expectations to conform to.
